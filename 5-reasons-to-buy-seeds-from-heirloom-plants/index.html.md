# 5 Reasons to Buy Seeds from Heirloom Plants for Your Garden

I’m all for keeping traditions going and I like being able to show my kids seeds of plants that were raised by my great-great grandparents. These same seeds have provided my family with beautiful plants for many years. The best way to keep traditional plants thriving is to purchase heirloom seeds. Here are 5 reasons to buy seeds from heirloom plants to cultivate a resilient, flavorful, and sustainable garden for generations to come.

## 5. To Have Genetic Diversity

Some types of commercial plants are experiencing a significant loss of genetic diversity, which means they are far more susceptible to blight, pests, and shifting climate conditions. Having a wide variety of plant genetics to choose from can help home gardeners find crops best suited for their particular growing location and soil conditions. Preserving genetic diversity through heirloom varieties helps safeguard our food supply against unexpected environmental challenges. Organizations like the [Seed Savers Exchange](https://www.seedsavers.org/) work tirelessly to protect these historic seed varieties.

Here are key benefits of maintaining rich plant biodiversity:

- Higher natural resilience against local garden pests and diseases
- Adaptability to specific microclimates and soil profiles
- Unique flavor profiles and vibrant visual appeal unmatched by commercial strains

## 4. Heirloom Seeds Aren’t Sterile

Many first generation hybrid seeds (F1 hybrids) are hand-pollinated and produce seeds that are either sterile or will not grow true to the parent plant in subsequent generations. That isn’t so with open-pollinated heirloom seeds. Some commercial seed companies produce hybrid seeds so that gardeners continually have to purchase new seeds each season, ensuring ongoing sales. Choosing open-pollinated heirloom seeds gives you full seed sovereignty, enabling you to harvest and re-plant your own seeds year after year while maintaining [genetic diversity](#5) in your backyard plot.

## 3. Genetic Engineering Hasn’t Taken Place in Heirloom Seeds

The DNA of most genetically engineered seeds (GMOs) has been altered in laboratory settings over the years for commercial agricultural traits. Heirloom seeds sold today are open-pollinated strains that remain virtually identical to the parent plants harvested decades or centuries ago. Any alterations that have occurred over time have taken place naturally through standard insect or wind pollination. If you want pure, traditional crops free from laboratory modification, heirlooms are the gold standard. You can learn more about agricultural standards from the [USDA](https://www.usda.gov/).

| Seed Attribute | Heirloom Seeds | Hybrid Seeds (F1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pollination Method | Open-pollinated naturally | Hand cross-pollinated |
| Seed Reproducibility | Fertile & true-to-type | Often sterile or variable |
| Genetic Modification | None (100% Non-GMO) | Standard or hybrid bred |

When comparing seed types, understanding these fundamental differences empowers you to make informed decisions for your home garden plot.

## 2. More Varieties of Heirloom Seeds Exist

First generation hybrid seeds offer very few commercial varieties to choose from, whereas heirloom seeds for a single crop type—like tomatoes, beans, or squash—can feature thousands of historic varieties. I love having lots of choices when searching for seeds of any kind, from rare purple tomatoes to sweet historic melons. Exploring diverse heirlooms allows you to discover [open-pollinated seeds](#4) with incredible historic heritage and unmatched kitchen quality.

## 1. You Can Help Keep Heirloom Plants Going for Many More Years

The more heirloom seeds are purchased and planted, the more likely these historic plant lines will continue to produce high-quality seeds for years to come. Gardeners have a unique opportunity to grow the exact same plant varieties their ancestors once cultivated to nourish their families. By saving and sharing seeds, you help [keep heirloom plants going for many more years](#1) and build a living connection to agricultural history.

I hope these 5 reasons spark an interest in you to go out and purchase at least a couple types of heirloom plants or seeds for your next growing season. If you already grow heirloom varieties in your garden, what made you choose them and which ones are your absolute favorites?
